Understanding Market Quotes and Yield Curves

Key Concepts

Market quotes are the raw data used to construct financial models, especially yield curves. Understanding how different instruments contribute to the curve is crucial.

Definitions:
  • Deposit Rate: Short-term interest rate for deposits, anchoring the very short end of the yield curve.
  • Futures Price: Implied interest rate from futures contracts, providing information for the short to medium term.
  • Swap Rate: Fixed rates exchanged in interest rate swaps, used to calibrate the longer end of the yield curve.
  • Zero Rate: The yield of a hypothetical zero-coupon bond for a given maturity. It represents the return an investor would get from a zero-coupon bond.
  • Discount Factor: The present value of one unit of currency received at a future date. It's derived directly from the zero rates.
  • Forward Rate: The implied interest rate for a future period, calculated from the current yield curve. It tells us what the market expects future interest rates to be.
Nelson-Siegel Model

The Nelson-Siegel model is a widely used parametric model to fit yield curves. It describes the yield as a function of maturity using four parameters:
y(τ) = β₀ + β₁ * (1 - e^(-τ/λ)) / (τ/λ) + β₂ * ((1 - e^(-τ/λ)) / (τ/λ) - e^(-τ/λ))
Where:

  • y(τ) is the yield for maturity τ
  • β₀ represents the long-term level of the yield curve.
  • β₁ represents the short-term slope.
  • β₂ represents the medium-term curvature.
  • λ is a decay parameter that determines where the hump in the curve occurs.
This model is favored for its ability to capture the typical shapes of yield curves (upward sloping, downward sloping, humped) with a small number of parameters.

Bond Price Change Methodology

Bond prices are inversely related to interest rates. When the yield curve shifts, bond prices change. This section illustrates how a benchmark bond's price reacts to a hypothetical parallel shift in the yield curve. A positive shift in rates generally leads to a decrease in bond prices, and vice-versa. The magnitude of this change depends on the bond's duration and convexity.
